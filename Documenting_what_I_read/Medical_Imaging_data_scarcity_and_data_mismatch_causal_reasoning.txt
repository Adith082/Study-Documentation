1. mediators
Sunlight (A) → Skin cell damage (B) → Skin cancer (C).

Here, skin damage mediates the effect of sunlight on cancer.

Once you know the skin is damaged, you don’t need to separately consider sunlight to predict cancer risk.

2. (b) Confounder
--------------------
B<-----A----->D

A causes both B and D.

Example:

Sunlight (A) causes Vitamin D (B) and also Skin damage (D).

If you just look at Vitamin D and skin damage, they’ll appear linked, but it’s really sunlight that causes both.

So sunlight is the confounder.



3. (c) Collider

A----->B<------E

Two different things both cause the same outcome.

Example:

Sunlight (A) and Genetics (E) both affect Skin cancer (B).

At first, sunlight and genetics are unrelated.

But if you only look at people with cancer (conditioning on B), sunlight and genetics suddenly look related — because if someone has cancer without sunlight, it’s probably genetics, and vice versa.

This is called a collider effect.



Big picture

Causal reasoning lets scientists:

Go beyond “A and B are linked” → to “A actually causes B.”

Decide what to control for in studies.

Ask what if questions, like:

“What happens if we give vitamin D supplements?”

“What if we reduce sunlight exposure?”

This is what makes causal models more powerful than just statistical correlations




If the task is causal (X → Y) like segmentation (images → masks), then unlabeled images don’t add much value.

Why? Because the way we draw masks (Y) only depends on what’s already in the image (X). More raw images without masks don’t help you learn how people annotate.

If the task is anticausal (Y → X) like skin lesion classification (disease → image appearance), then unlabeled images can help, since the disease (Y) leaves patterns in the image distribution (X).




Example — Prostate cancer segmentation:

Doctors outline tumor regions (labels) by looking at the MRI (input).

If you only have tons of MRI scans but no labels, you don’t really learn new information about how doctors draw the outlines.

So SSL isn’t fundamentally useful here.

But in practice…

SSL isn’t completely useless, even in causal tasks.

It can still help regularize the model, reduce overfitting, or cut down how much labeled data you need — but it won’t magically replace labels.

Evidence from research:

A big study found that well-tuned supervised models or transfer learning often beat SSL methods.

Worse, SSL can even hurt performance when the labeled and unlabeled data come from different distributions (e.g., one hospital’s images have more cancer cases than another’s).

🔹 Key Takeaways (layman’s terms):

Segmentation (causal tasks): more unlabeled images won’t fundamentally help, since the labels depend only on the image.

Classification (anticausal tasks): unlabeled images can help, because the disease changes how the image looks.

In practice: SSL might still give small practical benefits, but supervised learning + transfer learning are often stronger and more reliable.

Caution: Be careful when mixing labeled and unlabeled data from different sources (distribution mismatch).

💡 Analogy:
Imagine teaching someone to recognize handwriting.

If the task is “copy this handwriting style” (segmentation = causal), just giving them more blank paper (unlabeled input) won’t help — they need examples of how letters are drawn (labels).

If the task is “guess the writer’s mood from their handwriting” (classification = anticausal), then even seeing more unlabeled handwriting gives useful clues about patterns.

1. Invariance Augmentations

Definition: The model’s prediction should not change even if certain transformations are applied to the input.

Examples:

Histogram adjustments (brightness, contrast, intensity shifts).

Adding noise.

Geometric transforms like rotations, scaling, elastic deformations (for classification tasks).



2. Equivariance Augmentations

Definition: The model’s prediction should change in a corresponding way when the input is transformed.

Examples:

For segmentation/localization tasks, if you flip or rotate the image, the segmentation mask or bounding box must also flip/rotate accordingly.

Prostate tumor example (segmentation): If you mirror the MRI scan, the segmentation mask of the tumor must also mirror.

The text is about causal reasoning fundamentals in machine learning, and it introduces three levels of tasks:

Prediction

Goal: Use observed data to guess missing information.

Example: Classify an image as “tumor” or “not tumor.”

This is what most ML models do.

Intervention

Goal: See what happens when we actively change something.

Example: In a drug trial, force one group to take the drug and compare outcomes.

This is “doing” rather than just “observing.”

Counterfactual analysis

Goal: Ask “What would have happened if things were different?”

Example: If this patient had received chemotherapy instead of surgery, would they have survived longer?

Needs both observed data + a structural causal model.

👉 Most people think causal inference only matters in RCTs or experiments, but the authors argue: even prediction tasks in medical imaging sometimes require causal reasoning.










Think of a causal diagram as a map of “who causes what” in your data.

Steps:

Step 1: Pick the variables.

These are the “things that matter.”

Example in medical imaging:

Image itself

Patient’s health status

Diagnosis label (benign/malignant)

Annotation policy (how radiologists labeled images)

Hospital site

Image post-processing software

They don’t all have to be directly measurable — you can include abstract concepts (like “annotation policy”) if they’re important for explaining the process.

Step 2: Add arrows.

An arrow = “causes.”

No arrow = you assume no direct causal effect.

Example:

Disease → Image appearance (the tumor actually changes how the scan looks).

Image appearance → Doctor’s label.

Step 3: Granularity (detail).

You can draw the chain step by step (Disease → Tissue change → Image pixels → Label).

Or you can simplify it (Disease → Label).

Depends on how detailed you need the explanation to be.

Step 4: Add domain/environment info.

Example: Hospital A vs Hospital B (different scanners, patient demographics).

These “domain indicators” are shown as special variables with arrows going out.

If you don’t draw an arrow from “hospital” to “image,” you are assuming images look the same regardless of hospital (which might be wrong).

Step 5: Add selection variables.

Sometimes the dataset doesn’t include everyone, only a subset.

Example: only patients who agreed to biopsy are included.

You’d draw a “Selection” node and connect arrows from the variables that influenced who got included.

Step 6: Iterate.

After making the diagram, check: Does it really reflect reality? If not, fix it.

Goal: balance accuracy (don’t omit important stuff) with clarity (don’t overcomplicate).



2. Interpreting a causal diagram

Why do we bother? Because diagrams reveal biases.

Bias = fake correlation caused by hidden paths.

Two main classic types:

Confounding bias = a hidden common cause.

Example: Smoking causes both yellow teeth and lung cancer → makes it look like yellow teeth cause cancer.

Collider bias = conditioning on a common effect.

Example: Hospital admissions depend on both disease and age. If you only look at admitted patients, you may create spurious links between age and disease.

Dataset shift as bias:

Suppose you train a model using data from two hospitals.

Hospital A has younger patients, Hospital B has older patients.

If you don’t include “Age” as a variable, your model might accidentally think “hospital differences” explain the image differences.

Adding “Age” blocks that hidden path: Hospital → Age → Image.

This is what “controlling for” a variable means: cutting off fake pathways.

✅ Simple analogy:

Imagine you want to build a causal diagram for ice cream sales.

Variables: Weather, Temperature, Ice Cream Sales, Sunglasses Sales.

Arrows: Weather → Temperature → Ice Cream Sales. Also, Weather → Sunglasses Sales.

If you don’t include “Weather” in the diagram, you might wrongly conclude Ice Cream Sales cause Sunglasses Sales.

Adding “Weather” blocks that fake path — now you see both are just caused by weather.

🎯 Summary in plain words

Building a causal diagram = list all important factors, draw arrows for causes, and decide how detailed you want to be.

Interpreting diagrams = look for hidden paths that create fake correlations (biases), and fix them by including the right variables.

In medical imaging, this helps explain dataset shift (e.g., hospital differences, selection of patients) and ensures models don’t pick up misleading patterns.s
